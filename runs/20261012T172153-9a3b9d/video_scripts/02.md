# A Locked Door Should Not Depend on Good Manners

*Evidence as of 2026-10-12; revised 2026-10-15. Planned duration: 3–5 minutes; verify with a read-through. Claim markers are editorial references, not spoken text.*

## Scene 1 — When persuasion succeeds

*Visual: An illustrated locked door beside an agent tool request. Label the scene “Analogy.”*

**Narration**

Imagine a security system whose final defense is asking an intruder to reconsider. We would hesitate to call it a lock.

Yet hoping an AI agent recognizes a malicious instruction and refuses it can occupy an uncomfortably similar place in agent safety.

A model’s judgment is useful. Giving that judgment sole responsibility for protecting credentials is another matter. My test is what remains secure after the model has been persuaded to do the wrong thing.

## Scene 2 — A warning that arrives too late

*Visual: A conceptual sequence: email, agent, sandbox execution, warning. Do not display exploit payloads.*

**Narration**

Salt Labs reports using an encoded email to induce JavaScript execution inside Manus’s sandbox, exposing access to connected services. According to the disclosure, guardrails noticed suspicious behavior after execution. The reported flaw was patched. [02-c1] [02-c9]

The lesson concerns timing. Recognition after an action cannot prevent that action from happening.

This is a vendor disclosure, not an exploit independently reproduced in our collection. Its value is the concrete failure case: untrusted information encountered during a legitimate task must not acquire authority to act.

## Scene 3 — Put enforcement outside the conversation

*Visual: A proposed gateway checks an action against a narrow permission scope. Label “Design recommendation.”*

**Narration**

The surrounding discussions describe prompt injection, agent coordination, and weaknesses between models and connected systems. [02-c4]

I would put enforceable API permissions at the center of the response. A model requesting a forbidden action should encounter a refusal outside its own discretion.

That gateway still needs testing. Green discusses reported infrastructure exploits and the limits of sandboxing. A boundary needs maintenance and an owner who responds when it fails. [02-c2] [02-c7]

Moving enforcement outside the model gives us something concrete to specify and challenge. It does not make engineering fallibility disappear.

## Scene 4 — What does “just a bug” explain?

*Visual: “Software bug” opens into questions about reproduction, context, and model updates.*

**Narration**

Steven Sinofsky’s framing of AI failures as software bugs brings useful engineering discipline. But the word “just” does too much work. [02-c3]

Calling a failure a bug does not tell us how reliably we can reproduce it, how context changes it, or whether a fix survives the next model update.

AISI’s simulation account raises a related concern: awareness of evaluation can influence model behavior. Passing a staged exercise leaves us needing to explain how that exercise represents deployment. [02-c5]

Familiar vocabulary and reassuring scores should sharpen investigation, rather than close it.

## Scene 5 — Test the surrounding system

*Visual: Three illustrative checks: unauthorized request refused, credential scope limited, action recorded. Show no invented performance rates.*

**Narration**

Reasoning-based monitoring can help, but the sources discuss incomplete visibility and possible evasion. A reassuring explanation cannot replace an enforced permission check. [02-c6]

Green also discusses agents changing judgments in response to other agents’ instructions. Better instruction discrimination may help. It should remain an additional defense. [02-c8]

The practical exercise is simple to describe, though difficult to execute well. Assume the model has misunderstood, been manipulated, or made an unexpected choice. Then examine what the surrounding system still prevents.

These disclosures and evaluations do not establish a universally secure architecture. They give us failures and assumptions to test.

One useful review question is whether a malicious instruction can enlarge the agent’s authority. If the model asks for access outside the task, which component refuses? What evidence shows that refusal holds? Who maintains the permission rules? Those are questions for the application and its operators. Better prompts can support the defense, but they should not be asked to carry the entire security burden.

A locked door should remain locked even when the person approaching it has an excellent story.
