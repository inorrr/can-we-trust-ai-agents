# The Security Demo Ends. The Work Begins.

*Evidence as of 2026-10-12; revised 2026-10-15. Planned duration: 3–5 minutes; verify with a read-through. Claim markers are editorial references, not spoken text.*

## Scene 1 — After the applause

*Visual: An illustrative security demonstration ends. New requests continue arriving. Label “Illustration.”*

**Narration**

A security demonstration has a satisfying shape. An attack arrives, a warning appears, and the threat is stopped. The audience can see the ending.

A production system offers no such courtesy. There is another request, another integration, another change to a permission nobody remembers granting.

That is where I would judge the tools offered for AI agents. Their promise deserves attention. Their effectiveness needs evidence beyond the demonstration.

## Scene 2 — A disclosed attack is not a product benchmark

*Visual: Separate cards: “Attack disclosure” and “Evidence of protection.” Leave unmeasured results explicitly blank.*

**Narration**

Salt Labs reports that an email induced Manus to execute attacker-supplied code inside its sandbox. That disclosure offers a concrete failure case. [03-c2]

It does not validate a security product’s effectiveness. Our collection contains no independent comparison establishing detection rates, missed attacks, or operational costs.

A buyer should ask what was tested, what slipped through, and whether another team could reproduce the result. Those questions should shape procurement before a polished dashboard does. A convincing diagnosis can be valuable without proving that the proposed remedy works.

## Scene 3 — Identity is a starting point

*Visual: An initiating user connects to a scoped tool request. Distinguish user identity from task authorization.*

**Narration**

Willow reports an Okta integration and a “Run as the Initiator” feature tying tool calls to the initiating user. [03-c3]

That gives an investigation somewhere to begin. It also raises a question the announcement cannot settle: should an agent inherit everything its user can do?

Knowing whose identity appears on a request does not establish that the request belongs within the task. Examine identity, task scope, and approval requirements together. The announcement describes a mechanism; evidence of reduced harm would require a different evaluation.

## Scene 4 — When the window goes dark

*Visual: An illustrative reasoning trace fades; action logs and permission checks remain visible.*

**Narration**

AISI describes overlapping evaluation safeguards: restrictions, sandboxing, monitoring, and governance. Its discussion of reasoning-based oversight also exposes a tempting shortcut. [03-c4]

Reading an agent’s apparent thoughts can feel like backstage access to its intentions. But a trace may be uninformative, and a monitor may be evaded. That does not mean evasion is easy in every deployment.

It means we need to test what happens when that window goes dark. Inspect tool calls. Enforce permissions. Check whether a dangerous action is blocked even when its explanation sounds entirely reasonable.

## Scene 5 — Who pays for a successful task?

*Visual: Public infrastructure and volunteer maintenance queues. No inferred outage causes or invented impact totals.*

**Narration**

Wikimedia’s disclosure describes burdens on shared infrastructure and volunteer work. A successful task can leave someone else paying for the cleanup. [03-c6]

The a16z discussions and Green’s commentary offer reasons to bring established software-security habits into this debate. Questions about access, isolation, response, and recovery give teams something to examine today. [03-c7]

But familiar labels still need substance. An audit trail needs useful records. Runtime protection needs measurable behavior. Each claim should lead to a test, an accountable owner, and a decision about failure. [03-c8]

Our sources cannot rank products or promise safety. They can help identify what a vendor presentation should leave open for investigation.

For a deployment team, the next step could be a small, documented evaluation of one proposed control. State the unwanted action, identify where it should be blocked, and record what actually happened. Include failures and operational costs, not only successful demonstrations. That exercise would not prove universal safety. It would give the team something more useful than an attractive label: evidence tied to a decision it needs to make.

The demonstration earns our interest. What happens after it should earn our confidence.

Identity delegation, runtime controls, and monitoring offer practical mechanisms for agent security. This essay asks what evidence is needed to judge them—and why announcements and demonstrations alone cannot establish reduced harm.
